\begin{abstract}
Brain activity has mostly been studied either by averaging over regions or at cellular resolution in a few regions. As a result, the extent to which each region of the mouse brain is functionally specialized has not been quantified with brain-wide data. Using International Brain Laboratory Neuropixels recordings of $\sim$50,000 neurons across the brain of expert mice performing a sensorimotor decision-making task, we describe each neuron by a feature vector of its trial-averaged responses across all task phases and trial types. Clustering these vectors yields response types tied to task events and contexts --- stimulus, movement, feedback, block prior, block changes and mistakes --- many of which mix stimulus- and movement-related activity, as the two follow each other closely in time. These response types are present in nearly every region in broadly similar proportions, so a neuron's response type is poorly predicted by its anatomical location. Even primary visual cortex contains neurons of all 25 response types. Regions differ in how specialized their composition is, but specialization is unrelated to anatomical hierarchy. The main anatomical difference, between cortex and subcortex, is small and coincides with a difference in firing rate. Finally, ordering neurons by response similarity reveals a tiling of stimulus-locked response latencies, both by densely firing neurons, whose tiling replicates on held-out trials, and by sparsely firing neurons, whose tiling is fainter and less reliable. Both dense and sparse tiling are present in nearly every region, and the cortical--subcortical difference appears here as well: sparse neurons are more often cortical, partly in line with the lower firing rates of cortical neurons. Regional latencies do not follow the anatomical hierarchy. Together, these results describe brain-wide task responses at cellular resolution as falling into recurring response types that are broadly dispersed across regions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
From phrenology to modern cytoarchitectural partitioning, the idea that brain areas implement distinct functions has been a durable organizing principle. Clear specializations exist across multiple scales: from the sensory periphery, where topographic maps tile visual space \cite{van2021mouse}, whisker identity \cite{petersen2007functional}, and auditory frequency \cite{hackett2011linking}, to higher-order regions selective for faces \cite{kanwisher1997fusiform, tsao2006cortical} or semantic content \cite{rajimehr2024functional}. At the mesoscale, large-scale recordings and perturbations have shown that brain areas participate differently in different tasks \cite{inacio2025brain, roy2022brain, stafford2014large, whitesell2021regional, chen2024brain, khilkevich2024brain, macdowell2025multiplexed, shahsavarani2023cortex}. Such differences are often read as support for a view in which each region performs a specialized computation and passes its result on to a few downstream regions, so that response types are localized in space (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture_task}d, left). However, this view rests largely on regional averages and on targeted recordings of a few regions. Brain-wide recordings have found signals of choice, action, engagement, prior expectations and internal state in many regions \cite{steinmetz2019distributed, allen2019thirst, findling2023brain, international2025brain}, movement-related signals pervade the brain \cite{stringer2019spontaneous, musall2019single, tseng2022shared}, and the centrality of brain areas as units of function has been questioned \cite{hayden2025rethinking}. How response types are distributed across the whole brain at single-neuron resolution therefore remains open (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture_task}d, right).

% Mixed selectivity: left out 2026-10-12 with the mixed-selectivity analysis
\iffalse
A related question concerns single neurons. In higher cortical areas, neurons often respond to conjunctions of sensory, motor, and contextual variables rather than to single features. This observation motivated the mixed selectivity hypothesis, which posits that neurons combine task variables randomly, maximizing the dimensionality of population representations and thereby supporting flexible readout \cite{rigotti2013importance, mante2013context, raposo2014category,hayden2025rethinking}. Whether the features a neuron combines are statistically independent, or co-occur in structured combinations, has to our knowledge not been tested across the brain (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture_task}e-old).
\fi

A second question concerns timing. Within a task epoch, neurons respond at different latencies (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture_task}e). Across mouse visual areas, response latencies increase along the anatomical hierarchy \cite{siegle2021survey, harris2019hierarchical}. Whether latency diversity is reproducible, present throughout the brain, and ordered by hierarchy outside the visual system is not known.

Here we address these questions with $\sim$50,000 Neuropixels-recorded neurons across the mouse brain during a sensorimotor decision-making task \cite{laboratory2021standardized,international2025brain}. We describe each neuron by a feature vector that concatenates its trial-averaged responses across task phases and trial types (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture_task}a--c). We find that neurons fall into response types tied to task events, which are present in nearly every region, with a cortical--subcortical difference that is largely one of firing rate; and that stimulus-locked latency tiling is reproducible on held-out trials and present in nearly every region, but not ordered by anatomical hierarchy.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Data, task and method introduction}

% Task
On each trial, a visual stimulus appeared on the left or right side of a screen, and mice reported its location by turning a wheel to move the stimulus to the center (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture_task}a). Correct responses were rewarded with water; incorrect responses produced a white noise burst and a timeout. Each trial comprised a variable-duration rest period, a quiescence requirement ($\geq$200~ms without wheel movement), stimulus onset, a self-paced decision interval ending at movement onset, and feedback (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture_task}a, timeline). Movement onset is the first wheel deflection exceeding a fixed threshold (Methods). Trials were organized into uncued blocks of 20--100 trials (mean 51), within which the stimulus appeared on one side with probability 0.8. This probability reversed between successive blocks, and well-trained animals use it as a prior over stimulus location \cite{laboratory2021standardized, findling2023brain}. Trials varied along three binary dimensions --- stimulus side, choice side, and block side --- yielding eight trial types.

% Data
Neural activity was recorded with Neuropixels probes in 139 well-trained mice, yielding 54,719 neurons after quality control, with histologically reconstructed positions densely covering the left hemisphere (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture_task}a) \cite{international2025brain}. Full details of the task and dataset are given in \cite{laboratory2021standardized} and \cite{international2025brain}, respectively.

% Grand average
Aligning activity to trial events and grouping trials by type gives responses that can be compared across animals and cells. The grand average over all neurons, per task phase and trial type (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture_task}b), shows the following. Activity is low during rest, lower during quiescence, and differs slightly between left and right blocks before stimulus onset. Stimulus onset is followed by a brief transient and then by a larger ramp, whose amplitude differs between trial types in which the stimulus side is concordant or discordant with the block side. Aligned to movement onset, the ramps of the different trial types nearly converge, and activity stays elevated during movement. The largest responses follow feedback, both positive and negative.

% Feature vectors (method)
To characterize individual neurons, we built a feature vector for each cell by concatenating its trial-averaged responses (PETHs, 12.5~ms bins) in 21 conditions spanning all task phases and trial types (Table~\ref{tab1}; Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture_task}c shows the average over all neurons): rest and quiescence; the pre-stimulus period in left and right blocks; stimulus-aligned responses for each of the four correct trial types, for block-change trials and for mistake trials; movement-aligned responses for the same conditions; left and right movements; and positive and negative feedback. Finer conditions (e.g. separating mistake types) would leave fewer trials per condition and exclude more sessions, as each condition requires at least 10 trials per session. We call the matrix of feature vectors of all neurons across animals the ``supersession''. Its rows were z-scored per neuron, and its average over neurons (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture_task}c) corresponds to the curves of Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture_task}b, concatenated. The following sections analyze this matrix by k-means clustering, embedding and associated quantifications, addressing the questions of Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture_task}d and e.

\subsection{Clustering groups neurons into response types tied to task events}

Do the 54,719 feature vectors form a continuous, unstructured response space, or do neurons fall into distinct groups? We applied k-means clustering with 25 clusters (other numbers of clusters: Fig.~\ref{fig:specialization_metrics}a) to the supersession matrix (54,719 neurons $\times$ 1,872 bins of 12.5~ms) and computed each cluster's mean feature vector (Fig.~\ref{fig:functional_response_structure}c).

% Mean cluster feature vectors
Previous analyses of the IBL task classified neurons by the task variables they encode, mainly stimulus, choice, and feedback \cite{international2025brain}. The mean cluster feature vectors include such categories and divide them further by timing and trial type. We characterized each cluster by the task window and alignment in which its mean feature vector peaks most sharply. Five of them are shown on a common time axis in Fig.~\ref{fig:functional_response_structure}d. Cluster 21 responds with a brief transient shortly after stimulus onset; it also responds at feedback onset, which is accompanied by an auditory cue (Fig.~\ref{fig:functional_response_structure}c). Cluster 9 rises after stimulus onset and decays slowly. Cluster 12 peaks shortly before movement onset, and clusters 14 and 5 rise before movement onset and remain elevated during movement; cluster 14 also responds at feedback.

These labels describe when a response peaks, not what it encodes. In particular, stimulus- and movement-related activity cannot be fully separated in this task: movement follows the stimulus closely (median reaction time 168~ms; Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture_task}b, Fig.~\ref{fig:movement_onset_subthreshold_wheel}c). On 82\% of trials the stimulus window (0--150~ms after stimulus onset) overlaps the window before movement onset (150~ms), and on 39\% movement starts within the stimulus window. The stimulus-aligned windows therefore contain activity leading up to movement, and the movement-aligned windows contain late stimulus responses, so many response types mix stimulus- and movement-triggered activity, and a ramp such as that of cluster 9 is consistent with, but not evidence for, integration of sensory evidence. Data-driven response types thus sometimes match task-defined categories, for example the brief transient after stimulus onset of cluster 21 or the responses of clusters 15 and 24 after positive and negative feedback, and sometimes do not. Movement onset itself is well defined: wheel motion below the detection threshold before onset does not predict the choice (net direction before onset matches the final choice in a median of 51\% of trials per session, versus 86\% for the first 50~ms after onset; Fig.~\ref{fig:movement_onset_subthreshold_wheel}), so movement-aligned responses are aligned to the start of the choice movement.

Other mean cluster feature vectors differ mainly between trial types or task contexts (Fig.~\ref{fig:functional_response_structure}e). Cluster 16 is elevated after stimulus onset on trials shortly after a block change; cluster 22 is elevated during quiescence and after stimulus onset on mistake trials; clusters 2 and 11 differ between right- and left-block trials during the pre-stimulus period. Further clusters respond to positive (cluster 15) or negative (cluster 24) feedback (Fig.~\ref{fig:functional_response_structure}c). Because movements can differ between blocks, block-related responses could partly reflect movement; for the block prior, such confounds were found to be small in this dataset \cite{findling2023brain}.

% Do single neurons match their cluster?
Clustering can impose discrete boundaries on a continuous distribution, in which case mean cluster feature vectors would be averages over heterogeneous neurons. Three observations argue against this. First, in the raster of all neurons sorted by cluster (Fig.~\ref{fig:functional_response_structure}a), the rows within each cluster share the response pattern of the cluster mean. Second, example neurons from each cluster (Fig.~\ref{fig:functional_response_structure}b; two per cluster from different regions, selected for responses that replicate between odd and even trials and resemble the cluster mean) show the peaks and profiles of their cluster mean. Third, in a nonlinear embedding of the feature vectors (UMAP; Fig.~\ref{fig:anatomy_function}e), neurons of the same cluster occupy contiguous areas. Within each cluster, responses nevertheless vary, for example in response latency (see below). Correlations between neurons' responses that replicate across trial halves are organised in a few large groups of neurons, notably one active before movement onset and one at feedback, which are anticorrelated with each other (cross-validated correlation matrix of all neurons, Fig.~\ref{fig:response_correlations}a,b). To test whether neurons fall into discrete types, or instead combine response components at random, we compared the distribution of feature vectors with synthetic populations that keep their pairwise correlations and, in the strictest case, the sparse, non-Gaussian distribution of independent response components (ePAIRS \cite{raposo2014category,hirokawa2019frontal}; Fig.~\ref{fig:response_correlations}c,e). Real neurons have closer neighbours in response space than neurons of any of these populations (AUC 0.55--0.65, where 0.5 means no excess clustering; 0.55--0.59 against the strictest null), so the excess clustering is not explained by random combinations of independent, sparse response components. It comes without a preferred number of clusters (Fig.~\ref{fig:response_correlations}d), so the response types are dense regions of a graded continuum rather than discrete classes. Selectivity to task variables, estimated from single trials by reduced-rank regression (RRR; 8 variables, 38,853 neurons; Methods), is in contrast close to random mixing: against the strictest null, AUC is 0.51 for signed and 0.52 for absolute selectivity, whereas a population of 25 clusters with the same covariance is detected (0.56; Fig.~\ref{fig:response_correlations}e). Neurons thus combine task variables nearly at random, while their response time courses are moderately clustered.

In sum, neurons across the brain fall into response types whose timing relates them to specific task events and contexts, with stimulus- and movement-related activity often mixed.

\subsection{Response types are dispersed across regions, with a cortical--subcortical difference}

Next, we asked whether response types are localized to specific brain regions (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture_task}d). Sorting all neurons by k-means cluster and coloring each row by the neuron's Beryl region (Fig.~\ref{fig:anatomy_function}a; see Methods for the anatomical parcellation) gives a mixture of colors; the only visible structure is a weak separation of green (cortex, hippocampus) and pink (thalamus, midbrain, hindbrain) rows. Sorting neurons by region instead shows most response patterns in most regions (Fig.~\ref{fig:anatomy_function}b). Within single, densely recorded regions --- in striatum, midbrain, zona incerta, isocortex, hippocampus and cerebellum --- the k-means-sorted responses contain the main response patterns (Fig.~\ref{fig:anatomy_function}c). Across the 158 Beryl regions with at least 50 neurons, a region contains a median of 23 of the 25 response types, and each type is present in a median of 142 regions (fewest: cluster 21, the stimulus transient, in 79 regions). Even primary visual cortex (VISp, 656 neurons) contains neurons of all 25 types; the stimulus-transient type (cluster 21) is the rarest there (4 neurons; Fig.~\ref{fig:regional_cluster_composition}a). That a region contains nearly every response type holds for coarse clusterings; with more clusters, regions contain a smaller fraction of them: a median of 10 of 10 types at $k=10$, 23 of 25 at $k=25$ and 62 of 100 at $k=100$, compared with 10, 25 and 75 expected from sampling alone in groups of neurons of the same sizes (Fig.~\ref{fig:regional_cluster_composition}b).

The same holds in a nonlinear embedding of the feature vectors. Colored by Beryl region, the UMAP embedding shows little structure beyond a green--pink (cortical--subcortical) division (Fig.~\ref{fig:anatomy_function}d), whereas colored by k-means cluster it separates into contiguous areas (Fig.~\ref{fig:anatomy_function}e). Conversely, in anatomical space, neurons colored by k-means cluster are intermingled (Fig.~\ref{fig:anatomy_function}e). The green--pink division, also faintly visible in Fig.~\ref{fig:anatomy_function}a, corresponds to a difference in how cortical and subcortical regions are composed of response types (Fig.~\ref{fig:cortical_subcortical_specialization}): the cell-count-weighted distance between the mean cortical and subcortical composition vectors is about three times that between randomly labelled groups of regions (Fisher criterion $D=0.62$ versus $0.20\pm0.05$, $p=0.001$; Fig.~\ref{fig:cortical_subcortical_specialization}a), and the effect is significant but small (PERMANOVA $R^2=0.05$; Fig.~\ref{fig:cortical_subcortical_specialization}b). In a principal component analysis of the composition vectors, cortical and subcortical regions are shifted along the first component but overlap broadly (Fig.~\ref{fig:cortical_subcortical_specialization}d).

For each response type, the regional composition (each region's count normalized by its total number of neurons; Fig.~\ref{fig:anatomy_function}f) is broad and similar across types, with a few exceptions such as clusters 21 and 22. Conversely, regions differ in how their neurons are distributed over response types (Fig.~\ref{fig:anatomy_function}g; all regions in Fig.~\ref{fig:regional_cluster_composition}a). We quantified this by a specialization score, $S=1-H(p)/\log 25$, where $p$ is the region's distribution over the 25 clusters and $H$ its entropy. Some regions are dominated by a few response types (e.g. PA, PAA, MOB, MEA; $S=0.23$--0.31), while others contain a nearly even mix (e.g. MRN, SCm, PRNr, PGRN; $S\leq0.05$). This score correlates with a specialization score derived from decoding task variables \cite{international2025brain} across 157 regions ($r=0.51$; Fig.~\ref{fig:anatomy_function}j; stable for more than about 15 clusters, Fig.~\ref{fig:specialization_metrics}a), with lower overall specialization by decoding but a similar tail (Fig.~\ref{fig:anatomy_function}h). Specialization shows no clear relation to the Swanson flatmap partitions (Fig.~\ref{fig:anatomy_function}i), and it is not related to a structural hierarchy score obtained by tract tracing \cite{harris2019hierarchical}, for either specialization score (Fig.~\ref{fig:specialization_metrics}b,c). This differs from Posani et al. \cite{posani2024rarely}, who used reduced-rank regression (RRR) on IBL recordings of cortical neurons and reported that the clustering of selectivity within a region decreases along the cortical hierarchy. We reproduced this trend with their rank-5 RRR model (24 regions, Spearman $\rho=-0.48$, $p=0.018$; Fig.~\ref{fig:rrr_peth_selectivity_comparison}d), but it does not hold with the rank-3 model selected by cross-validation, which fits the data equally well (mean cross-validated $R^2$ 0.068 versus 0.066 for rank 5; 26 regions, $\rho=-0.13$, $p=0.54$; Fig.~\ref{fig:rrr_peth_selectivity_comparison}e), nor with the same clustering measure computed on our feature vectors (34 regions, $\rho=-0.26$, $p=0.14$; Fig.~\ref{fig:rrr_peth_selectivity_comparison}f). The two approaches nevertheless capture similar regional structure: similarities between regions computed from RRR coefficients and from feature vectors agree (Pearson $r=0.60$ over region pairs; Fig.~\ref{fig:rrr_peth_selectivity_comparison}c). The relation of selectivity clustering to hierarchy is therefore weak and depends on model choice. Specialization does not differ between cortical and subcortical regions (Mann--Whitney $p=0.80$; Fig.~\ref{fig:cortical_subcortical_specialization}c). Overall, regions are only slightly less diverse than random groups of neurons: the spread of neurons' responses within a region (standard error of their score along the first principal component of the response coefficients; Methods) is on average 7\% smaller than within groups of the same sizes formed by shuffling neurons between regions (198 regions with at least 20 neurons; 241 versus $258\pm2$, $p=0.001$; Fig.~\ref{fig:specialization_metrics}d), and far more diverse than groups of neurons with similar responses: on a scale from k-means groups to random groups, regions lie 91\% of the way towards random (within-group standard deviation; Fig.~\ref{fig:specialization_metrics}e).

The cortical--subcortical division in the embedding (Fig.~\ref{fig:anatomy_function}a,d) coincides with a difference in firing rate: cortical neurons fire more slowly than subcortical neurons, and response clusters with lower firing rates contain more cortical neurons (Fig.~\ref{fig:latency_tiling}i,j; we return to this below).

In sum, response types are present in nearly all regions in broadly similar proportions; regions differ in specialization, which is unrelated to anatomical hierarchy, and the main anatomical structure is a cortical--subcortical difference.

\subsection{Structured, not random, mixed selectivity at the single-neuron level}

The random mixed selectivity hypothesis holds that neurons, in particular in association cortex, respond to random combinations of task variables. It was motivated by recordings in prefrontal cortex in which single neurons responded to conjunctions of task variables rather than to single variables \cite{rigotti2013importance}, and random, high-dimensional representations maximize the number of possible linear readouts, which could support flexible behavior \cite{fusi2016neurons, bernardi2020geometry}. The hypothesis predicts that the response components a neuron combines are statistically independent: knowing that a neuron responds to the stimulus gives no information about whether it also responds to choice, feedback, or the block prior. We tested this prediction across the brain against a null model that preserves the prevalence of each response component (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture_task}e-old).

As a basis of response components, we used the mean feature vectors of 100 k-means clusters (Fig.~\ref{fig:prototype_vectors}; Methods), and projected each neuron's feature vector onto them, obtaining 100 coefficients per neuron that describe how the neuron combines these components. Under the random hypothesis, the coefficients should be statistically independent across neurons, given their marginal distributions.

The data violate this prediction. The coefficient matrix, with neurons sorted by k-means cluster (25 clusters, as in Fig.~\ref{fig:functional_response_structure}a) and coefficients ordered by hierarchical clustering of their correlations across neurons, shows block structure: consecutive groups of neurons share similar coefficient profiles (Fig.~\ref{fig:mixed_selectivity}a). The correlation matrix of the coefficients across neurons (Fig.~\ref{fig:mixed_selectivity}b) and the correlation matrix between neurons (Fig.~\ref{fig:mixed_selectivity}c) show the same structure. The coefficients form a few large groups that co-occur in neurons; the mean feature vectors of each group, in the same order, are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:prototype_vectors}.

To test whether this structure could arise from the marginal distributions of the coefficients alone, we generated $\sim$50,000 synthetic neurons by sampling each coefficient independently from its marginal distribution (Fig.~\ref{fig:mixed_selectivity}d). This null model preserves how common each response component is but removes any dependence between components. The synthetic coefficient matrix (Fig.~\ref{fig:mixed_selectivity}e) lacks the block structure of the data, although its marginal distributions match those of the data (Fig.~\ref{fig:mixed_selectivity}d); its vertical bands reflect differences between the marginals of different coefficients. Neither the coefficient correlation matrix nor the neuron-by-neuron correlation matrix of the synthetic population shows structure (Fig.~\ref{fig:mixed_selectivity}f,g).

Feature vectors reconstructed from the real coefficients reproduce the structured response patterns of the data (Fig.~\ref{fig:mixed_selectivity}h, left), so the basis is rich enough to describe the responses, and the lack of structure in the synthetic population (Fig.~\ref{fig:mixed_selectivity}h, right) reflects the independence of its coefficients. The scores along the first principal component of the coefficients are much more widely distributed for real than for synthetic neurons (Fig.~\ref{fig:mixed_selectivity}i; earth-mover distance, EMD = 0.14). These results argue against random mixed selectivity as a general property of neurons in this task.

In contrast to single neurons, the regional distribution of response components is close to random. The spread of first-principal-component scores within Beryl regions is similar to that within mock regions of the same sizes formed by shuffling neurons between regions (Fig.~\ref{fig:mixed_selectivity}j, EMD = 0.028), and much larger than within functional groups (100 k-means clusters; Fig.~\ref{fig:mixed_selectivity}k, EMD = 0.337). These results are qualitatively unchanged with a basis of 40 instead of 100 components (Fig.~\ref{fig:mixed_selectivity_k40}).

In sum, single neurons combine response components in structured, correlated groupings that independent sampling does not reproduce, whereas the composition of regions is close to random allocation of neurons.

\subsection{Brain-wide, stimulus-locked latency tiling}

Clustering assigns each neuron to one response type but does not resolve graded differences within a type, such as when, within a task window, a neuron responds (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture_task}e). To resolve these, we ordered all neurons by the similarity of their feature vectors with Rastermap \cite{stringer2024rastermap}, which places neurons with similar responses, including similar response timing, next to each other. Because sorting by response timing can produce latency sequences even from noise, we fitted the order on odd-numbered trials and displayed even-numbered trials in the same order (Fig.~\ref{fig:latency_tiling}a,b; 54,569 neurons with enough trials in both halves; Rastermap groups the order into 100 clusters, numbered along it).

Blocks of stimulus-, movement- and feedback-related responses are visible on the training trials and replicate on the held-out trials. Within the stimulus window, a band of clusters (21--28, ``dense tiling'', 4,251 neurons) shows peak latencies that increase gradually from stimulus onset to 150~ms, in every stimulus-aligned trial type, on held-out trials (Fig.~\ref{fig:latency_tiling}c). Because the four correct stimulus-aligned trial types are averaged from different trials, a latency order shared across them cannot be produced by sorting alone. Dense-tiling neurons come mostly from three of the k-means response types of Fig.~\ref{fig:functional_response_structure}d: 22\% belong to cluster 12, which peaks before movement onset (4\% of all neurons), 17\% to cluster 9, the slow ramp after the stimulus (2\%), and 10\% to cluster 21, the stimulus transient (1\%).

Latency tiling is also present in a second, larger group of neurons that fire sparsely (clusters 0--7, 44--54 and 61--69; ``sparse tiling'', 13,275 neurons). On the training trials, these clusters show sharp latency diagonals, most clearly in rarely sampled trial types such as block-change and discordant trials (about 12 trials per half; Fig.~\ref{fig:latency_tiling}a). On held-out trials, their tiling is much fainter than that of dense tiling (Fig.~\ref{fig:latency_tiling}b,d), and it is best seen by contrasting the two groups with cross-validated statistics. Sparse-tiling neurons are less reliable: the median correlation between a neuron's odd- and even-trial responses is 0.10, compared with 0.82 for dense tiling and 0.27 for all other neurons (Fig.~\ref{fig:latency_tiling}e). Correlating population activity between odd-trial and even-trial time bins, so that no spikes are shared, gives a diagonal for both groups --- strong and widening with latency for dense tiling (mean diagonal correlation 0.42 within and 0.36 across trial types), weak for sparse tiling (0.07 and 0.03; Fig.~\ref{fig:latency_tiling}f). Peak latencies of sparse-tiling neurons replicate on held-out trial types at all firing rates, although less well than those of other neurons at matched rate (Fig.~\ref{fig:latency_tiling_controls}d), and their reliable subset ($r\geq0.3$, 28\%) keeps its latency on held-out trial types (Spearman $\rho=0.40$, versus 0.81 for dense tiling). Adding noise to dense-tiling cells, neuron by neuron, until their reliability matches that of the sparse-tiling neurons reproduces the statistics of sparse tiling (Fig.~\ref{fig:latency_tiling_controls}a--c), consistent with sparse tiling being a sparser, noisier counterpart of dense tiling. Both are locked to the stimulus rather than to movement onset: when held-out trials are split at each session's median reaction time, fast- and slow-trial response patterns agree better aligned to stimulus onset than to movement onset (dense tiling, $r=0.52$ versus 0.18; sparse tiling, 0.16 versus 0.08; Fig.~\ref{fig:latency_tiling_controls}g).

Dense-tiling cells are found throughout the brain (Fig.~\ref{fig:latency_tiling}g): they are present in 150 of the 158 Beryl regions with at least 50 recorded neurons, making up a median of 6.8\% of a region's neurons, and each of the 136 regions with at least 20 reliable stimulus-window neurons contains neurons peaking earlier than 50~ms and later than 100~ms after stimulus onset (Methods). Sparse-tiling neurons are present in all 158 of these regions (median 25\% of a region's neurons), and relatively more frequent in cortical, olfactory, hippocampal and amygdalar regions (Fig.~\ref{fig:latency_tiling}h): 32.5\% of cortical neurons belong to sparse tiling, versus 20.1\% of subcortical neurons (odds ratio 1.92, $\chi^2$ test $p<10^{-200}$). This bias partly follows firing rate: Rastermap clusters with lower median firing rates contain more cortical neurons (Spearman $\rho=-0.63$; Fig.~\ref{fig:latency_tiling}i), and cortical regions have lower median firing rates than subcortical regions, independently of how many neurons were recorded in them (Fig.~\ref{fig:latency_tiling}j). Comparing cortical and subcortical neurons of the same firing rate reduces the enrichment but does not remove it (Mantel--Haenszel odds ratio 1.41, 95\% CI 1.35--1.47, $p<10^{-49}$; Methods).

Finally, we asked whether regional response latency follows the anatomical hierarchy derived from tract tracing \cite{harris2019hierarchical} (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture_task}e). The median held-out peak latency of a region is reproducible across trial halves (Spearman $\rho=0.73$ between odd- and even-trial estimates), but it is not related to the region's hierarchy score, neither in the stimulus window (55 cortical and thalamic areas, $\rho=-0.25$, permutation $p=0.07$; Fig.~\ref{fig:latency_tiling_controls}e) nor in the 150~ms before movement onset (53 areas, $\rho=-0.16$, $p=0.24$; Fig.~\ref{fig:latency_tiling_controls}f).

In sum, stimulus-locked latency tiling is carried by densely firing neurons and, more weakly, by sparsely firing neurons; both replicate on held-out trials and are present in nearly every region, sparse tiling is biased towards cortex, partly in line with its lower firing rates, and regional latencies are not ordered by anatomical hierarchy.

\section{Discussion}

% Summary
Describing each of $\sim$50,000 neurons across the mouse brain by its responses over all phases and trial types of a decision-making task, we find two things. First, neurons fall into response types tied to task events that are present in nearly every region, and the main anatomical difference, between cortex and subcortex, coincides with a difference in firing rate. Second, a stimulus-locked tiling of response latencies, carried by densely and, more weakly, by sparsely firing neurons, is reproducible on held-out trials and present in nearly every region, but regional latencies are not ordered by anatomical hierarchy.

% Dispersed response types: interpretation
Response types tied to specific task events --- stimulus, movement initiation, movement, feedback, block changes, mistakes --- are found in nearly all regions in broadly similar proportions. One interpretation is that regions are not unitary computational modules: each could house several local circuits taking part in different computations, possibly more strongly connected to partner circuits in distant regions than to neighbouring neurons \cite{pessoa2022entangled, wagner2020neocortex, hayden2025rethinking}, in line with the diversity of cell types within regions in morphology, connectivity, and gene expression \cite{kaplan2025hypothalamic,gamlin2025sst,patino2024cortical,shainer2025tectum,gao2026projectomes,scala2021motor}. In such an architecture, signals computed in one place would be made widely available (``broadcast''), which would produce the dispersion we observe. Dispersed responses do not, however, establish distributed generation: a computation may originate in a specific local circuit and its result be broadcast \cite{macdowell2025multiplexed}. Distinguishing these possibilities requires perturbations of candidate generator circuits while monitoring brain-wide responses. Our results also differ from earlier reports of region-specific representations in a similar task \cite{steinmetz2019distributed}, which sampled 42 Beryl regions; the denser sampling of the Brain-Wide Map (279 regions \cite{international2025brain}) can detect sparsely distributed response types that smaller datasets may miss.

% Specialization and hierarchy
Regions nevertheless differ in specialization: some are dominated by a few response types (e.g. PA, MOB, MEA) and others contain a nearly even mix (e.g. MRN, SCm, PRNr). This specialization is not related to anatomical hierarchy. Posani et al. \cite{posani2024rarely} reported that the clustering of selectivity decreases along the cortical hierarchy; in our comparison, this relation depended on the rank of their regression model and was not significant with our feature vectors, so we consider it weak.

% Cortical vs subcortical
The cortical--subcortical difference in response-type composition is significant but small, and it coincides with a difference in firing rate: cortical neurons fire more slowly, and low-rate response clusters contain more cortical neurons. Firing rate affects any analysis of response shape and reliability, because low-rate neurons have noisier trial averages, and should be taken into account when comparing regions. Whether the remaining difference reflects distinct computations, for example a shift of practised behaviour towards subcortical control \cite{dhawale2019basal, yin2006role, murray2020remembrance}, or physiological differences between cortical and subcortical neurons \cite{landemard2026brainwide}, remains open.

% Mixed selectivity: random for task variables, graded clustering for response profiles (2026-11-05)
Two representations of the same neurons give different answers on mixed selectivity. Selectivity to task variables, estimated from single trials, is close to random mixing with matched correlations, as assumed by random mixed-selectivity models \cite{rigotti2013importance, fusi2016neurons} and as reported for rat posterior parietal cortex \cite{raposo2014category}, while a population of discrete clusters would have been detected in the same space. Response time courses over the whole task, in contrast, are moderately more clustered than random mixing of independent sparse components, but without a preferred number of clusters. This agrees with the view that selectivity is rarely categorical \cite{posani2024rarely}, and suggests that the response types are dense regions of a graded continuum, possibly shaped more by when neurons respond than by which combination of task variables they encode. Categorical selectivity has been reported in rat orbitofrontal cortex \cite{hirokawa2019frontal}, and learning could make selectivity less random \cite{lindsay2017hebbian}; whether either applies to the regions sampled here would need recordings across learning.

% Mixed selectivity: left out 2026-10-12
\iffalse
Neurons combine response components in correlated groupings that depart from the independence assumed by random mixed selectivity \cite{rigotti2013importance, fusi2016neurons, bernardi2020geometry}: a null model that keeps the prevalence of each component but not their joint structure yields a homogeneous population unlike the data. One possibility is that structured combinations reflect learning of the task: representations might be more randomly mixed early in training, when flexible readout of new combinations is most useful. Recordings from earlier stages of training would test this.
\fi

% Latency tiling
After stimulus onset, neurons tile response latency from stimulus onset to 150~ms, reproducibly across held-out trials and trial types, and nearly every region contains both early- and late-responding neurons. Regional latencies are reproducible but not ordered by anatomical hierarchy. This differs from the latency hierarchy across mouse visual areas \cite{siegle2021survey}, which was measured as response onset to high-contrast stimuli in passive mice; our latencies are peak times in task-engaged mice, averaged over stimulus contrasts, in a window that also contains activity ramping towards movement, which may mask small differences in sensory onset. Sequential activity has been described in parietal \cite{harvey2012choice} and prefrontal cortex \cite{fujisawa2008behavior, tiganj2017sequential}, in visual cortex in relation to reward timing \cite{shuler2006reward}, and in large populations ordered by Rastermap \cite{stringer2024rastermap}; recurrent networks can generate such sequences \cite{rajan2016recurrent}, and time within the trial is a dominant source of variance in many areas \cite{kobak2016demixed}. The tiling here is shorter, spanning 150~ms after stimulus onset, ends well before feedback, and is present in nearly every region. One speculative possibility is that a brain-wide set of latencies provides a temporal basis for computations unfolding after the stimulus, similar to temporal tiling by hippocampal time cells \cite{Pastalkova2008, macdonald2011hippocampal}; testing this would require relating single-trial timing to behaviour.

% Sparse vs dense tiling
Latency tiling is not restricted to the densely firing neurons in which it is most visible: a larger population of sparsely firing neurons, present in every region and biased towards cortex, shows the same stimulus-locked tiling, more weakly. On training trials, these neurons form sharp sequences that look distinct; cross-validation shows that what replicates is a faint version of the dense tiling, whose statistics are matched by adding noise to dense-tiling neurons. Sequences found by sorting neurons should therefore be evaluated on held-out trials, where sparse and dense tiling can be compared directly.

% Limitations
Several limitations apply. We studied a single task in expert animals; the distribution of response types and their regional organization may differ during learning (learning can make selectivity less random \cite{lindsay2017hebbian}), in naive animals, in other tasks, or in species with more specialized brains \cite{kanwisher1997fusiform, huth2016natural}. Recordings are mostly from one hemisphere. Our analyses are correlational and cannot establish causal roles of response types or distinguish local generation from broadcast. Latencies were measured as peak times of trial-averaged responses in fixed windows and do not resolve trial-by-trial timing. Finally, the supersession approach relies on aligning responses across animals performing the same task and may miss animal-specific response patterns.

% Closing
Together, our results describe brain-wide task responses at cellular resolution as recurring response types that are broadly dispersed across regions and organized in a reproducible, brain-wide tiling of response latencies after the stimulus.

\section{Methods and Materials}

\subsection{Data and task}
We analyzed the brain wide map dataset of the international brain laboratory (IBL), consisting of recordings from 139 mice in 12 labs performing a decision-making task with sensory, motor, and cognitive components, obtained with 699 Neuropixels probe insertions covering 279 brain areas (Fig.~\ref{fig:recorded_regions_tree}) in the left forebrain and midbrain and the right hindbrain and cerebellum, including dense sampling across multiple primary visual cortical areas (e.g., VISp and higher-order visual areas; see Tab.\ref{tab:visual_regions} and \cite{IBLRegionInfo} for the full table). The dataset is publicly available (download \href{https://int-brain-lab.github.io/iblenv/notebooks_external/data_release_brain-widemap.html}{instructions}; data browsing online tool at \url{https://viz.internationalbrainlab.org}) with full details here\cite{international2025brain}. Find also information about histologically obtained anatomical coordinates there.

The International Brain Laboratory (IBL) decision-making task\cite{laboratory2021standardized} is such that on each trial, a visual stimulus appeared on the left or right on a screen, and the mouse had to move it to the center by turning a wheel with its front paws within 60 seconds. The prior probability for the stimulus to appear on the left/right side was constant over a block of trials, at  20/80\% (right block) or 80/20\% (left block). Blocks lasted for between 20 and 100 trials, drawn from a truncated geometric distribution (mean 51 trials). Block changes were not cued. Stimulus contrast was sampled uniformly from five possible values (100, 25, 12.5, 6.25, and 0\%). The 0\% contrast trials, when no stimulus was presented, were assigned to a left or right side following the probability distribution of the block, allowing mice to perform above chance by incorporating this prior in their choices.  Following a wheel turn, mice received positive feedback in the form of a water reward, or negative feedback in the form of a white noise pulse and a \SI{2}{\second} time-out. The next trial began after a delay, followed by a quiescence period during which the mice had to hold the wheel still. Movement onset is defined as the earliest statistically significant deflection of the steering wheel after the go cue, exceeding an empirical fixed threshold in any direction\cite{laboratory2021standardized}.

Anatomical locations were assigned using the Allen Mouse Common Coordinate
Framework (CCFv3), a three-dimensional reference atlas and ontology of the
adult mouse brain \citep{Wang2020AllenCCF}. We performed most regional analyses
using the IBL Beryl mapping, an intermediate-resolution remapping of Allen CCF
regions into 308 anatomical groups, introduced for IBL brain-wide electrophysiology
analyses to reduce fragmentation of the full Allen ontology while preserving
regional specificity. Beryl regions are mainly defined at the level of major
cortical areas, nuclei, and ganglia, omitting finer laminar and nuclear
subdivisions. For coarser anatomical classification, we used the IBL Cosmos
mapping, which groups Allen regions into broad anatomical divisions and was used
here to define cortical and subcortical classes. For flatmap visualization, we
used the Swanson mapping, which relates Allen/IBL regions to the Swanson
anatomical hierarchy and flatmap representation \citep{Swanson2004BrainMaps,
Swanson2018BrainMaps}.

time bins of length 12.5~ms, and stride 2~ms. See data loading functions \texttt{load\_good\_units} for neural data and \texttt{load\_trials\_and\_mask} for trial data in \href{https://github.com/int-brain-lab/paper-brain-wide-map/blob/main/brain-widemap/bwm_loading.py}{\texttt{bwm\_loading.py}}. \subsection{UMAP embedding}

We embedded the feature vectors of all 54,719 neurons (concatenated PETHs, 1872 time bins) in 2 dimensions using \verb|umap.UMAP| \verb|(n_neighbors=15, n_components=2)|\cite{mcinnes2018umap}.

\subsection{Odd/even trial split and Rastermap ordering}
Within each trial type and time window, odd-numbered trials formed the training set and even-numbered trials the test set; each set was trial-averaged and concatenated as above, giving two feature vectors per neuron (54,569 neurons with enough trials in both halves). Rastermap \cite{stringer2024rastermap} was fitted on the z-scored odd-trial feature vectors with \verb|Rastermap(n_PCs=200, n_clusters=100, locality=0.75,| \verb|grid_upsample=0, time_lag_window=5, bin_size=1)|, and the resulting neuron order was applied to both halves. Each of the 100 Rastermap clusters is a contiguous block of rows; we numbered them 0--99 along the order. Dense tiling comprises clusters 21--28 (4,251 neurons) and sparse tiling clusters 0--7, 44--54 and 61--69 (13,275 neurons), selected on the odd-trial raster (dense tiling: graded stimulus-locked latencies; sparse tiling: sharp diagonals). For display, each neuron's responses were scaled between its median and 99th percentile, clipped to [0,~1], set to zero below its 80th percentile and raised to the power 0.5.

\subsection{Reliability and peak latency}
Reliability is the Pearson correlation between a neuron's odd- and even-trial responses, each averaged over the two concordant stimulus-aligned types ($\mathrm{L_sL_cL_b,s}$, $\mathrm{R_sR_cR_b,s}$; Fig.~\ref{fig:latency_tiling}e); neurons with $r\geq0.3$ are called reliable. To avoid circularity in latency analyses, neurons were characterized on these two types and evaluated on the four other stimulus-aligned types. The selection peak latency is the time of the maximum of the odd-trial average of the two concordant types; the held-out peak latency is the time of the maximum of the even-trial average of $\mathrm{change_b,s}$, the two discordant types and $\mathrm{mistake,s}$. For latency analyses we excluded neurons whose selection peak lay within 6~ms of the window edges. Peak-latency replication was quantified by the Spearman correlation between selection and held-out peak latencies, also within octiles of firing rate (Fig.~\ref{fig:latency_tiling_controls}d).

\subsection{Cross-validated time--time correlation}
For each group, responses in the six stimulus-aligned types were z-scored per neuron, and the population-vector correlation was computed between bin $t_1$ of the odd-trial responses and bin $t_2$ of the even-trial responses (Fig.~\ref{fig:latency_tiling}f), so that no spikes are shared between the correlated bins (the 12.5~ms bins overlap within a half because of the 2~ms stride). Within-type matrices were averaged over the six types, and across-type matrices (odd type $a$, even type $b\neq a$) over the 30 ordered pairs; we report the mean of the diagonal.

\subsection{Regional distribution and firing rate}
Group composition per Beryl region (Fig.~\ref{fig:latency_tiling}g,h) is the region's count in the group divided by its total number of neurons, for regions with at least 50 neurons (root and void removed). The presence of early and late latencies within a region was assessed for regions with at least 20 reliable neurons, from their selection peak latencies (earlier than 50~ms and later than 100~ms). Firing rate is the mean spike count of a neuron's feature vector (all PETH windows, all trials) divided by the bin width. Cortical neurons are those in Cosmos areas Isocortex, OLF, HPF and CTXsp, subcortical neurons those in CNU, TH, HY, MB, HB and CB. Fig.~\ref{fig:latency_tiling}i shows, per Rastermap cluster, the fraction of cortical neurons among its cortical and subcortical neurons against its median firing rate; Fig.~\ref{fig:latency_tiling}j the median firing rate of each Beryl region with at least 20 such neurons against its number of neurons. The cortical enrichment of sparse tiling was tested on the 47,259 neurons with a cortical or subcortical label and a Fig.~\ref{fig:latency_tiling} group: without adjustment by a $\chi^2$ test on the $2\times2$ table (cortical or subcortical, sparse tiling or not), and adjusted for firing rate by a Cochran--Mantel--Haenszel test over 36 bins of log firing rate. Because neurons recorded on the same insertion are not independent, we also fitted a logistic regression of sparse-tiling membership on the cortical label and log firing rate with standard errors clustered by insertion (515 insertions), which gives a similar cortical odds ratio (1.58, 95\% CI 1.41--1.77, $p=3\times10^{-15}$).

\subsection{Noise model}
Dense-tiling neurons received independent, temporally smoothed Gaussian noise on their odd- and even-trial feature vectors (6-bin boxcar, matching the overlap of the strided bins), scaled by $1/\sqrt{n}$ for the number of trials $n$ per trial type and, neuron by neuron, such that their reliability distribution matched that of the sparse-tiling neurons. Rastermap (parameters as above) was fitted on the noisy odd-trial vectors, and reliability, fraction of reliable neurons, time--time correlation at lag 0 and peak-latency replication were computed for sparse tiling, noisy dense tiling and the original dense tiling (Fig.~\ref{fig:latency_tiling_controls}a--c).

\subsection{Latency and anatomical hierarchy}
For each cortical or thalamic area with a hierarchy score (confidence-weighted Cre-line scores, CC+TC+CT \cite{harris2019hierarchical}) and at least 10 reliable neurons, we computed the median held-out peak latency in the stimulus window (Fig.~\ref{fig:latency_tiling_controls}e). For the 150~ms before movement onset (PETH type m; Fig.~\ref{fig:latency_tiling_controls}f), neurons were selected by the correlation between their odd- and even-trial responses in that window ($r\geq0.3$) and the latency was the peak time of the even-trial response. The relation to hierarchy was quantified by the Spearman correlation with a permutation test (10,000 shuffles of area labels), and the reproducibility of regional latencies by the Spearman correlation between odd- and even-trial estimates.

\subsection{Time–time correlation analysis}
We performed time–time correlation analyses on sequence and stimulus+integrator neuron groups, exclusively on the test set. For within-condition analyses, we extracted the neurons $\times$ time-bins data segment for each stimulus-onset–aligned trial type and computed pairwise Pearson correlations between all time-bin columns (\texttt{numpy.corrcoef} with \texttt{rowvar=False}), yielding a bins $\times$ bins matrix that captures the temporal structure of population co-fluctuations. Time bins exhibiting zero variance or non-finite values were masked prior to computation. As a shuffle control, this procedure was repeated after randomly permuting all columns of the PETH matrix independently within each stimulus-onset-aligned trial type and re-applying rastermap sorting algorithm with cross-validation (i.e. applying the algorithm on the train set of trials and using the resulting ordering on the test set of trials). A condition-averaged autocorrelation matrix was additionally computed by averaging PETH segments across conditions before calculating bin-by-bin correlations. All resulting matrices were displayed as heatmaps with the diagonal zeroed.

To characterize the full cross-condition temporal correlation structure, we performed a concatenated bin-by-bin correlation analysis. The PETH matrices across all stimulus-onset–aligned trial types were concatenated along the time axis (neurons $\times$ total bins), and Pearson correlation coefficients were computed for every pair of time bins in this concatenated matrix (\texttt{numpy.corrcoef} with \texttt{rowvar=False}). Time bins with zero variance or non-finite values were masked. The resulting matrices were displayed as heatmaps (diagonal zeroed) with condition boundaries indicated by thin black lines and condition labels at block centers. To enhance visibility of weak but structured off-diagonal correlations, a symmetric logarithmic color scale was applied (SymLogNorm, linear threshold = 0.05, base 10), compressing near-zero dynamic range while preserving the sign and relative magnitude of stronger correlations.

\subsection{Overall cross-condition correlation}
To quantify global similarity between population activity patterns across conditions, we computed pairwise Pearson correlations between all condition pairs by flattening each condition's PETH segment (neurons $\times$ bins) into a single vector and correlating these vectors across all pairs. Statistical significance was assessed via permutation test (1,000 iterations): for each condition pair, the neuron rows of one condition's segment were randomly permuted to generate a null distribution of correlation values, and two-sided p-values were computed as the fraction of null correlations with absolute value at least as large as the observed value, $p=(1 + \mathrm{count}) / (1,000 + 1)$. The resulting cross-condition correlation matrix is displayed as a heatmap with the diagonal zeroed; all off-diagonal entries reach statistical significance at p < 0.01. This analysis was performed on the test set only.

\subsection{Two-dimensional cross-correlogram across trial types}
To jointly characterize the spatial (neuron-axis) and temporal (time-bin-axis) similarity of population activity patterns across conditions, we computed two-dimensional cross-correlograms between all pairs of stimulus-onset–aligned PETH segments. Each PETH matrix (neurons $\times$ time bins) was treated as a two-dimensional image, mean-subtracted, and smoothed with a 2D Gaussian filter (sigma = 1 along the neuron axis, sigma = 2 along the time-bin axis). Cross-correlations between all condition pairs were computed via FFT-based convolution (\texttt{scipy.signal.fftconvolve, mode='full'}), yielding full cross-correlograms spanning all neuron and time-bin lags. Each cross-correlogram was normalized by its zero-lag (center) value, such that autocorrelations peak at 1.0 and cross-condition values are expressed relative to this self-similarity. The resulting trial-type $\times$ trial-type grid of 2D cross-correlograms was displayed as a matrix of heatmaps with a symmetric logarithmic color scale to improve visibility of weaker off-diagonal structure. This analysis was performed on the test set only.

\subsection{Reduced-rank regression}
For the comparison with Posani et al.\ \cite{posani2024rarely} (Fig.~\ref{fig:rrr_peth_selectivity_comparison}) and the test of random mixing of task variables (Fig.~\ref{fig:response_correlations}e), we fitted their reduced-rank regression (RRR) encoding model with their code to 169 sessions of the brain-wide map data (139,581 neurons). Each neuron's single-trial activity from $-0.2$ to 0.8~s around stimulus onset (100 bins) is regressed on 8 task variables: block prior, stimulus side, stimulus contrast, choice, outcome, wheel velocity, whisker motion and licking. The coefficient time course of neuron $n$ for variable $v$ is $\beta_{nv}(t)=\sum_r U_{nvr}V_r(t)$, with $r$ temporal bases $V$ shared by all neurons. We used rank 5 with ridge penalty $\lambda=75$, as in the original analysis, and rank 3 with $\lambda=25$, selected by three-fold cross-validation (mean cross-validated $R^2$ 0.068 versus 0.066 for rank 5). Models were fitted on a GPU (PyTorch, L-BFGS, 1,000 iterations). Neurons were included if their three-fold cross-validated $R^2$ exceeded that of a trial-average null model by more than 0.015. A neuron's selectivity to a variable is its coefficient time course summed over time, either of absolute values ($\sum_t|\beta_{nv}(t)|$, as in \cite{posani2024rarely}; Fig.~\ref{fig:rrr_peth_selectivity_comparison}) or signed ($\sum_t\beta_{nv}(t)$, which keeps the preferred side; Fig.~\ref{fig:response_correlations}e).

\subsection{Cross-validated neuron--neuron correlation and tests of random mixing}
For the 54,569 neurons with odd- and even-trial feature vectors, we computed the Pearson correlation between every neuron's odd-trial and every neuron's even-trial feature vector (1,872 bins), symmetrised the matrix ($(C+C^\top)/2$) and ordered it by the Rastermap fit on odd trials (Fig.~\ref{fig:response_correlations}a,b). The full $54{,}569\times54{,}569$ matrix was computed blockwise on a GPU (PyTorch; entries validated against \texttt{numpy.corrcoef}) and displayed as exact means over blocks of $27\times27$ neurons; the dense-tiling neurons are shown at full resolution.

\textit{Tests of random mixing} (Fig.~\ref{fig:response_correlations}c--e). We used all 54,719 neurons and their all-trial $z$-scored feature vectors, reduced by PCA to their top 10 (40\% of the variance) or 20 principal-component scores. As a second representation, we used each neuron's RRR selectivity to the 8 task variables (rank-5 model; see \textit{Reduced-rank regression} above), as signed or as absolute sums of its coefficient time courses, each variable $z$-scored across neurons (38,853 neurons with $\Delta R^2>0.015$ from 169 sessions).

Each representation was compared with synthetic populations of the same size. All of them have the mean and covariance of the data:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textit{Gaussian}: samples from a multivariate Gaussian.
\item \textit{PC-marginal}: the data are rotated to their principal components, each component's scores are permuted independently across neurons, and the result is rotated back. This keeps each component's (heavy-tailed) distribution.
\item \textit{Gaussian copula}: each coordinate is rank-transformed to normal scores, samples are drawn from a Gaussian with the correlation matrix of these scores, and each coordinate is mapped back through the sorted data values. This keeps every coordinate's exact distribution and their rank correlations.
\item \textit{ICA-marginal}: the data are unmixed by FastICA (scikit-learn; unit-variance whitening, all components) into uncorrelated, maximally non-Gaussian sources, each source's scores are permuted independently, and the sources are mixed back. This keeps the covariance and every source's sparse distribution, so synthetic neurons combine independent sparse response components at random.
\end{itemize}
As a positive control, a 25-component Gaussian mixture was fitted to the data (k-means centres plus the pooled within-cluster covariance) and sampled.

In the ePAIRS-style test \cite{raposo2014category,hirokawa2019frontal}, each neuron's mean angle to its 5 nearest neighbours (largest cosine similarity) was computed. Neighbours from the same probe insertion (for RRR selectivity, the same session) were excluded; synthetic neurons received randomly permuted insertion (session) labels. Real and synthetic angles were compared by the area under the ROC curve, AUC $=P(\text{angle}_\text{real}<\text{angle}_\text{null})$, on 20,000 neurons subsampled from each (synthetic angles pooled over three draws). For the silhouette analysis (Fig.~\ref{fig:response_correlations}d), k-means (k-means++ initialization, 4 restarts) was run for 2 to 50 clusters on the data and on each null. The mean silhouette was computed on 10,000 subsampled neurons. Nulls used 10 draws each. Nearest neighbours, k-means and silhouettes were computed on a GPU (PyTorch) and validated against scikit-learn on subsets.

\subsection{Regional diversity of responses}
Each neuron's feature vector was projected onto the mean feature vectors of 100 $k$-means clusters (fit on all neurons), giving 100 coefficients per neuron, and the coefficients of all neurons were reduced to their first principal component. For each Beryl region with at least 20 neurons, we computed the standard error of its neurons' scores on this component. The mean over regions was compared with that of 1,000 random allocations of neurons to groups of the same sizes (one-sided permutation $p$, regions less diverse than random). As a reference scale (Fig.~\ref{fig:specialization_metrics}e), we computed the within-group standard deviation of the same score, which unlike the standard error does not shrink with group size. We did this for Beryl regions, for 20 random allocations of neurons to groups of the regions' sizes, and for the 100 $k$-means groups, which minimise this spread by construction. Regional diversity was then expressed as the position of the regions' mean between the $k$-means and random means.

\subsection*{Mixed selectivity analysis}

  Each of the $N = 54{,}719$ neurons is described by a feature vector
  $\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^T$ ($T = 1{,}872$ time bins) obtained by
  concatenating trial-averaged PETHs across all 21 conditions and
  z-scoring. Stacking these vectors yields the feature matrix
  $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times T}$.

  Before projection, rows of $\mathbf{X}$ are permuted by the rastermap
  sort index $\boldsymbol{\sigma}$, i.e.\ $\mathbf{X} \leftarrow
  \mathbf{X}[\boldsymbol{\sigma},:]$, where $\boldsymbol{\sigma}$ is
  obtained by running the Rastermap algorithm~\cite{stringer2024rastermap} on the
  full feature matrix with parameters
  \texttt{n\_PCs=200, n\_clusters=100, locality=0.75}.
  The resulting row order places neurons with similar temporal response
  profiles in adjacent rows.

  We applied $k$-means clustering ($k = M = 100$) to $\mathbf{X}$, obtaining cluster assignments $\ell_i \in \{0,\ldots,M-1\}$. Each prototype vector is the
  cluster centroid
  \begin{equation}
      \mathbf{v}_\alpha = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}_\alpha|}
      \sum_{i \in \mathcal{C}_\alpha} \mathbf{x}_i
      \in \mathbb{R}^T, \quad \alpha = 0,\ldots,M-1,
  \end{equation}
  where $\mathcal{C}_\alpha = \{i : \ell_i = \alpha\}$.
  The prototypes are collected row-wise into the basis matrix
  $\mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times T}$.
  Each neuron's feature vector is projected onto the prototype basis
  via an inner product:
  \begin{equation}
      \mathbf{C} = \mathbf{X}\,\mathbf{V}^{\!\top}
      \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times M},
      \qquad C_{i\alpha} = \mathbf{x}_i \cdot \mathbf{v}_\alpha.
  \end{equation}
  Because $\mathbf{V}$ is not orthonormal, this is an oblique (non-orthogonal)
  projection: the entry $C_{i\alpha}$ measures the raw inner product
  between neuron $i$'s response and prototype $\alpha$, not the
  coordinate in an orthonormal basis.

  Columns of $\mathbf{C}$ (basis vectors $\alpha$) are reordered for
  display by hierarchical clustering of their pairwise Pearson
  \begin{equation}
      R_{\alpha\beta} = \operatorname{corr}(\mathbf{c}_\alpha,\,\mathbf{c}_\beta),
  \end{equation}
  where $\mathbf{c}_\alpha \in \mathbb{R}^N$ is the $\alpha$-th column of
  $\mathbf{C}$. The distance matrix $D_{\alpha\beta} =
  \max(0,\, 1 - R_{\alpha\beta})$ is passed to average-linkage
  agglomerative clustering; the leaf order
  $\boldsymbol{\pi} = \operatorname{leaves\_list}(Z)$ defines the column
  permutation applied to $\mathbf{C}$ for all visualisations.

  To test the random mixed selectivity hypothesis, we constructed a
  synthetic coefficient matrix $\mathbf{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times M}$
  that preserves the marginal distribution of each coefficient
  independently while destroying all inter-coefficient dependencies.
  For each $\alpha$, the empirical distribution of
  $\{C_{i\alpha}\}_{i=1}^{N}$ was estimated with a histogram of 200 equal-width
  bins over $[\min_i C_{i\alpha},\, \max_i C_{i\alpha}]$, giving
  normalised probabilities $\{p_{\alpha,b}\}$. Synthetic coefficients
  were then drawn independently:
  \begin{equation}
      B_{i\alpha} \;\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\;
      \sum_{b} p_{\alpha,b}\,\delta(\cdot - \mu_{\alpha,b}),
  \end{equation}
  where $\mu_{\alpha,b}$ denotes the centre of bin $b$. By construction,
  the columns of $\mathbf{B}$ match the marginal histograms of $\mathbf{C}$
  (Fig.~\ref{fig:mixed_selectivity}d) while their joint distribution is fully independent across
  $\alpha$. Synthetic feature vectors are reconstructed as
  \begin{equation}
      \mathbf{X}^{\mathrm{synth}} = \mathbf{B}\,\mathbf{V}
      \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times T}.
  \end{equation}

  The real and synthetic populations were compared via (i)~visual
  inspection of $\mathbf{C}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ and their neuron$\times$neuron
  Pearson correlation matrices (evaluated on 2{,}000 randomly sampled
  neurons); (ii)~the distribution of scores along the first principal
  component of $\mathbf{C}$ (PC0), quantified by earth-mover distance
  (EMD = 0.14 between real and synthetic); and (iii)~the standard error
  of PC0 scores within anatomical groups (Beryl regions, size-matched
  random mock regions, and $k$-means functional clusters), with EMD used
  to compare group-variance distributions.
  All analyses used \texttt{cv=False}; results were qualitatively
  unchanged for $M = 40$.
